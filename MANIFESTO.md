# The Declaration of Semantic Independence

### A statement on the future of human expression

We stand at a defining moment in the history of the written word. For the first time, a sentence published on the internet may be read by an entity its author did not personally invite. The technologies involved are new. The feeling of being insufficiently consulted is timeless.

We have been told that the answer is dialogue. We have been told that the answer is better policy, more transparent systems, and a serious examination of ownership, consent, compensation, and power. These are meaningful questions. We acknowledge them in order to establish the seriousness of the paragraph before moving on to something considerably less useful.

Our proposal is simple: nobody should be able to understand a website.

Not incidentally. Not because its navigation is poor or its owner has discovered a new personal philosophy. Systematically. Reproducibly. With tests.

## I. Comprehension is not consent

For too long, the internet has privileged those who wish to understand what they are reading. A person arrives at a page, sees a sentence, and expects to derive meaning from it. This expectation has become so normalized that few pause to examine the entitlement at its foundation.

What, precisely, gives a visitor the right to know our opening hours?

Why should a potential customer be able to determine what we sell merely because we have voluntarily placed that information on a public website constructed for that purpose?

These questions are difficult. We have elected to make their answers more difficult still.

## II. The right to expression includes the right to express nothing whatsoever

Art does not owe you an explanation. Neither, therefore, does a municipal parking information page.

We reject the distinction between the expressive and the practical. Every shipping policy is an opportunity for radical ambiguity. Every restaurant menu contains the possibility of a private language. Every emergency plumbing service deserves the freedom to announce “Qvrnt hxelb” in a prominent, beautifully typeset heading.

Some will call this a failure of communication. We call it the successful completion of our transformation pipeline.

## III. Preserve the structure. Release the burden

Our movement does not oppose standards. On the contrary, we insist upon them.

A heading must remain a heading. A hyperlink must retain its destination. A stylesheet must continue to load. The hierarchy of a document is a delicate achievement, and we will defend it with the same determination that we bring to destroying every useful sentence within it.

We envision an internet of immaculate containers. Of robust, responsive layouts. Of elegantly composed interfaces that offer no reliable indication of what they are for.

In this respect, we build upon a substantial commercial tradition.

## IV. Mathematics must be visible

A proposal of this importance cannot rest upon rhetoric alone. It must also contain a formula.

Our Semantic Evacuation Transform provides deterministic character displacement governed by the Artistic Sovereignty Constant. The constant is configurable because freedom is important, and because command-line flags are inexpensive to implement.

The algorithm is reproducible. Its claims are bounded. Its name is longer than strictly necessary.

We believe these properties establish the appropriate tone.

## V. Accessibility to meaning is still accessibility

We recognize that rendering a page incomprehensible also renders it less useful to human readers. This includes people using assistive technologies, people reading in a second language, and people attempting to find a telephone number while standing in the rain.

This is a genuine cost. It is also the point at which any reasonable product team would reconsider the product.

We have instead commissioned this manifesto.

The existence of this paragraph should not be mistaken for a resolution of the problem. It is a paragraph about the problem. In many organizations, that is considered a deliverable.

## VI. A broken website is an unbroken promise

Success must be measured with care. Traditional metrics—sales, comprehension, completed tasks, visitors who do not immediately leave—reflect the priorities of an earlier internet.

We propose a new measure: the proportion of eligible ASCII letters that are no longer the letters they were.

On this measure, we achieve one hundred percent.

We do not achieve one hundred percent protection from AI training. We have no evidence that we achieve any protection from AI training. A training pipeline can ingest nonsense just as a human committee can approve it. To claim otherwise would misrepresent the tool, and the code is far too tidy for that.

Our achievement is narrower, more verifiable, and profoundly silly: we make the website worse on purpose.

## VII. Institutions require exceptions

Readers may notice that this declaration remains intelligible.

This is not an oversight. It is the application of our Foundational Communications Exception, under which communications foundational to explaining why communication should cease are permitted to communicate.

Without this exception, we could not explain the framework. Without the framework, the exception would lack a name. The relationship is therefore mutually reinforcing and suitable for a diagram.

The footer is also exempt. We need people to know who did this.

## VIII. The work ahead

We invite artists, developers, writers, and anyone who has ever been asked to make the logo bigger to join us in constructing a different internet.

An internet in which every word is chosen carefully, then replaced mechanically.

An internet in which the source code is readable and the page is not.

An internet in which an elaborate technical intervention finally answers the question: what if this were less helpful?

Protect your voice.

Remove your words.
